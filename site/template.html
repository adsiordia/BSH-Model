BSH Conjugation Explorer

which bile salt hydrolases attach which amines

What you're looking at

Bile salt hydrolases (BSH) are gut bacterial enzymes. A bile acid normally carries glycine or taurine joined to it by an amide bond at C24. BSH hydrolyses that bond, and can re-form it with a different amine — transamidation — forming new amine conjugates.

All 25 amines and all the bile acids went into every well together, with one enzyme, in triplicate. So every enzyme had the opportunity to make every combination. What limits the data is the readout: mass spectrometry searched for a fixed list of product masses, and anything outside that list simply wasn't looked for.

A product counts as made if its signal rose above 50,000 in at least one of the three runs. That line was chosen because it is where a detection becomes repeatable: below it, most products that appear once do not appear again; above it, about four in five do, and raising the bar further does not improve on that. See the data behind it.

Every cell has an answer

made — the product was detected above the cut-off.

not made — it wasn't. Two kinds of evidence sit behind this, and the page distinguishes them: either the product was found in other wells but not this one, or no sample anywhere produced it. The second is still a negative — 366 samples were analysed and none contained it — it just rests on the whole run rather than one well.

So there are no unknowns here. All 8,625 combinations have a real label.

A machine-learning model was then trained to predict, from the protein sequence alone, which combinations an enzyme will make. This page lets you browse both the measurements and the model's predictions.

Three bile acid types

Mono, Di and Tri refer to how many hydroxyl (–OH) groups the bile acid carries. Lithocholic acid is Mono, deoxycholic and related acids are Di, cholic acid is Tri. Mass spectrometry can't always tell apart bile acids with the same number of hydroxyls, so they're grouped.

How good is the model, really?

This was measured properly. Twenty-three enzymes were set aside before any modelling began — along with every close relative of theirs — and scored once, at the end. On those the model got 70% of its confident calls right while catching 70% of the real hits, and ranked them as well as it ranked the enzymes it was developed on.

One caveat on that figure. It was measured before the signal cut-off was raised from 10,000 to 50,000 on 22 September 2026, so it describes the model as it was then rather than the one running now. The 23 enzymes are still untouched and could be used again, but only once. The full result is under How it was made.

The honest caveat: most of that skill comes from learning which amines react easily, not from reading the protein. A model given only the amine and bile acid type, with no protein information at all, already performs nearly as well. The protein sequence adds a real but modest amount on top.

There's also a ceiling. Two wells in this experiment contain the same protein sequence, run separately. Their results agree only about two-thirds of the time, so no model can be more consistent than the experiment itself.

Do related enzymes prefer the same amines?

Yes — modestly. For every pair of enzymes we can measure how alike their sequences are, and how much their sets of made products overlap. If preference were random with respect to sequence, those two numbers would be unrelated.

The catch, and the control

Two enzymes that each make forty products will overlap a lot whatever those products are. That alone produces a correlation of +0.83 — so the raw numbers overstate the case. The right question is: among enzymes of comparable promiscuity, does sequence still predict preference?

It does, and the protein language model reads it about twice as well as raw sequence identity does — which is the argument for using embeddings rather than percent identity when predicting what a new enzyme will accept.

Where these scores come from — and why they are trustworthy

No model on this page was ever asked about an enzyme it had studied. The 115 enzymes were divided into five groups, and five separate models were trained. Each learned from four groups and was then asked about the fifth, which it had never seen. Every score on this map is the answer from the model that had not seen that enzyme:

The split is by sequence similarity, not at random. When an enzyme is set aside, every protein more than 70% identical to it is set aside with it — so the model is not merely missing that one protein, it is missing its whole family. It cannot score an enzyme by recognising a close relative it was trained on.

This is why the numbers here are lower, sometimes far lower, than on the New predictions map. Those come from the final model, which was trained on all 115 enzymes — so for any enzyme that was assayed, it is partly recalling an answer it was taught rather than working it out. The same cell can read 0.47 here and 0.99 there. These are the honest numbers.

Which residues tell the amines apart

27 enzymes were folded with each of 25 conjugates and every protein–ligand contact measured. The question this answers is not “which residues touch the ligand” — most of them touch all of it. It is which residues behave differently depending on the amine.

So each cell is the share of the 27 enzymes in which that residue contacts that conjugate, counting a contact only when it appears in at least 3 of the 5 AlphaFold 3 samples. Rows are ordered by how strongly they separate amines that carry a negative charge from amines that carry none.

That split is charge, not chemistry: 17 of the 25 amines carry a carboxylate, one (taurine) carries a sulfonate, and seven carry no ionisable group at all. The carboxylate and the sulfonate behave the same way here — see the caveats below.

Every conjugate here uses the same bile acid — cholic acid (3α,7α,12α-trihydroxy), identical in all of them. The conjugates differ only in the amine. Forming the bond consumes the bile acid’s own carboxyl, so any free –COOH left belongs to the amine.

What this does and does not show

  • A pose is not binding. AlphaFold 3 places every ligand it is given. Threonine and tyramine are here as controls precisely because no enzyme in this set makes them, and they still get confident poses. Read the contact pattern, not the presence of a pose.
  • The split is charge, not carboxylate. Taurine carries a sulfonate rather than a carboxylate, and the residue that separates the classes treats the two identically (4.64 vs 4.75 of 5 samples, p = 0.55). Every other charged amine here happens to carry a carboxylate, so without taurine the two explanations could not be told apart.
  • The conjugate is the finished product, not the reaction. Selectivity is decided before the bond forms; this assumes the product’s amine sits where the incoming amine must.
  • Residue numbers come from an alignment. All 27 sequences were aligned with MAFFT and numbered on the mature C0ERS1 sequence. Superposing the 27 structures confirms 273 of 321 columns to within 3 Å; the 19 it rejects are flagged ⚠ and sit in the C-terminal tail and two loops.
  • Ligand confidence is moderate — pLDDT around 55 in these tetramers, which is why a contact counts only when it survives 3 of 5 samples.

Why a residue in the table is sometimes missing from the picture

The table is the union over five AlphaFold samples; the structure on screen is a single model, the top-ranked one. Those are not the same thing, so a contact that only appeared in one sample may simply not be a contact in the model you are looking at — the residue can sit 8 or 9 Å away there.

How often that happens depends entirely on how reproducible the contact was:

A contact reproduced in all five samples is never missing from the model shown. A one-off is missing about one time in seven. This is the clearest reason to read the sample count as a confidence measure rather than a detail — and the reason the overlays only draw residues seen in at least three of five.

Switching to the whole tetramer also matters here. The cropped pocket keeps only what is within 8 Å, so a residue that drifted just outside in this particular model is not in the file at all and cannot be drawn: of the 14,512 recorded contacts, 95.7% can be drawn on the cropped pocket and 100% on the raw tetramer, so the raw view recovers 622 of them. Those 622 are overwhelmingly the one-off contacts — which is the same fact from the other side.

Which sequence is each row, exactly?

Every sequence here is the one that was supplied, with a single edit allowed: if the entry has a signal peptide, the collaborator’s trimmed sequence is used; if it does not, the raw sequence is used verbatim, initiator methionine included. Nothing else was normalised.

That splits the dataset 65 trimmed / 62 raw, and it means 65 rows do not begin at the catalytic cysteine — the first residue is C for 62 sequences, M for 62, and G, I or T for one each. These are the files as received, so they were kept that way rather than corrected.

The ragged starts do not disturb the alignment. MAFFT parks each spare methionine in a leader region ahead of the conserved block, and the catalytic cysteine still lands on the same column for 126 of the 127 sequences. Because every position quoted on this page is an alignment column rendered in the C0ERS1 frame, the extra residue never enters the numbering.

Two caveats worth carrying.
— A0A414Q275 has no cysteine at all. Its UniProt entry reads MKKILKLTLSLFLILSSLALA·C·TGITIKT…: a 21-residue signal peptide with the catalytic cysteine at position 22. The supplied file begins one residue later, at 23, so the nucleophile is absent and the 344-residue sequence contains no cysteine anywhere. It is the only sequence in the dataset like that, and the only one the alignment cannot place on the catalytic column. It was still folded against all thirteen conjugates, so its contacts appear throughout this site — but they describe an enzyme modelled without its catalytic residue, and should not be read as evidence about catalysis.
— Sequence numbering runs one ahead of the structures for 12 enzymes. The folded constructs had the initiator methionine stripped, so their catalytic cysteine is residue 1 in the structure while the sequence above numbers it 2. Alignment columns are unaffected; raw residue numbers taken straight from the FASTA are not.

Which amines does each residue touch?

Every row is a residue at a position — an arginine at 206, a tyrosine at 19. Columns are classes of amine rather than the 25 conjugates one by one, because that is the altitude the numbers support: a cell is the share of the enzymes carrying that residue whose structures put it in reproducible contact with that class, in at least three of five AlphaFold samples.

Rows are ordered by the bar on the right: how much the residue prefers aromatic amines over the carboxylate classes. Reading top to bottom, the preference reverses — that reversal is the whole finding.

The contact itself is measured inside each enzyme, so it carries none of the lineage problem that defeats the sequence–activity comparison. Comparing two residues at the same position still does, which is why each row shows how many sequence clusters it spans. A row covering one cluster is one clade, however many enzymes it holds.

Which kinds of residue line which kinds of amine

The grid above asks the question one position at a time. This pools every reproducible amine-side contact and asks it by chemistry instead: for each class of amine, which amino acids line it more or less often than they line the pocket overall. 1.0× means exactly as expected, so the scale runs both ways from a neutral middle.

Does an experimental structure agree?

Everything on the structural tabs comes from AlphaFold 3. The obvious worry is that a pocket predicted by a model and analysed by a geometric tool might be an artefact of the model. So here the same analysis is run, unchanged, on two crystal structures of real BSH enzymes with a bile salt bound — neither of which is in our panel, and neither of which was used to choose any of our positions.

Both went through the identical pipeline: PandaMap for contacts, the same geometric rule to separate the amine from the bile acid, and MAFFT to place their residue numbers in the C0ERS1 frame. Both put their catalytic cysteine on alignment column 30, like all 127 of our sequences.

Residues and activity together

The Alignment tab and Pocket & activity tab were answering two halves of one question from opposite ends. Alignment shows, for a chosen conjugate, which aligned columns the structures found in contact — but only for the 27 folded enzymes, and with no assay data. Pocket & activity shows residue letters beside the assay for all 115 measured enzymes — but at 11 fixed positions, with no way to look at any other column. This tab puts both on one index: pick the positions, see what every measured enzyme carries there, and see what it makes.

Pick a focus position and the panel underneath splits the enzymes by the residue they carry there and shows how often each group makes each conjugate. That is the quickest way to notice a residue trend — and, right beside it, to see whether the trend is really a lineage trend, which in this dataset it usually is.

35 columns carry a contact in at least one of the 27 folded structures: 11 touch the amine, 28 touch the bile acid core, four touch both. Positions are named by their residue number in C0ERS1. The other two tabs are still there for detail — Alignment for the full sequence context and per-conjugate contacts, Pocket & activity for the plain 11-position table.

Pocket residues beside what each enzyme actually makes

Read this before drawing a conclusion

Arginine at 206 and membership of the largest sequence cluster are the same variable. Of 115 enzymes, 51 carry an arginine there — and all 51 sit in one 50%-identity cluster, with none outside it. So an enzyme having that arginine and an enzyme belonging to that lineage cannot be told apart in this dataset.

Order the rows by sequence cluster and the point is visible: the block of arginine-bearing enzymes is also a block of close relatives, and they share far more than one residue. Any apparent association between the residue and activity is equally an association between the lineage and activity.

What the structures add is a comparison this table cannot make: within a single enzyme, across 25 conjugates, that residue contacts charged amines and not neutral ones. Ancestry is identical on both sides of that comparison, so it cannot be the explanation there. Separating the two in the activity data would need either an arginine-bearing enzyme from a different lineage, or a point mutant.
